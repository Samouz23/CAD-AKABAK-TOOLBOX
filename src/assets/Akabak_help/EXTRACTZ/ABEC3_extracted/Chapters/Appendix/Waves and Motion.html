Rigid Body Motion

In mechanics we often hear the term Rigid Body Motion. It means, an excursion, velocity or acceleration of the whole body. For example, a loudspeaker membrane should move in axial direction as a whole. In our context, we only regard sinoidal motion, say, forth and back, typically caused by mass-spring systems.

In mechanics the Rigid Body Motion has a direction. Accordingly and potentially there are a range of different Rigid Body Motions. A simple example is a translation in x- and y-direction. A more complicated is the rotational mode, for example a toggling cone-membrane. Two rotational modes can yield twisting, and so on.

For ABECs Lumped Element Analysis we only regard translational Rigid Body Motion.

Wave-Mechanics

Apart from the Rigid Body Motion there can be any set of wave-propagation. For example, a loudspeaker membrane moves forth and back and at the same time may perform all kinds of bending, shearing and stretching waves.

Potentially we have all kinds of motion. However, it depends whether a certain Rigid Body or Wave Motion is excited.

Rigid Body and Wave Motion are overlayed. This means that the total motion is the sum of both. For many electro-acoustic devices the mechanical Rigid Body Motion is dominant, and we can get fairly good simulation results by ignoring the more complicated wave-mechanics.

Wave-Length

In general an element or node can be regarded concentrated or lumped if we can identify meaningful terminals where potential and flow parameters are concentrated and are constant spatially, at least approximately.

For example, a Resistor of the electrical domain simply measures a constant resistance at its two terminals for the frequency range, which is typically used in electro-acoustics. However, at very high frequencies where the wave-length is comparable to the dimensions the same Resistor measures a complex impedance. It also matters how and where we mount the component. Which means, that at these frequencies the Resistor is not a lumped element anymore.
