Electro Dynamic Components, Lumped Element - Diaphragm - Derivation

An acoustic lumped element transducer features a diaphragm of some sort. The diaphragm couples the mechanical to the acoustical domain:

p = F/SD

V = v·SD

with F: force on the diaphragm, v: velocity of the diaphragm, p: sound pressure on the diaphragm, V: volume-velocity on the diaphragm, and

Za = Zm/SD2

with Za: lumped radiation impedance, Zm: lumped radiation impedance in mechanical terms and SD the effective diaphragm area.

All parameters stem from an integral over the diaphragm area. We say "lumped parameters" because local distributions are integrated and are part of the lumped value. In this way the Lumped Element model reduces any diaphragm-shape to an effective radiation area, SD.

See also chapter Lumped Element - Diaphragm - Derivation.

Diaphragm Parameters

Although the lumped element analysis calculates only with the diaphragm area SD, the LE-script offers the input of shape-related parameters, say dD= for the diameter of a circular diaphragm. This means all shape related issues are ignored, and there are no cones, domes or annuli for the lumped element part.

Note, as explained below, in the LE-script the specification of diaphragm parameters is only necessary if the component is not coupled to the Boundary Element Part.

For example, for a circular cone-shaped diaphragm we specify simply dD=, which should also include the suspension. Typically we add half of the suspension area assuming linear velocity distribution.

  Def_Driver  'Drv1'
    dD=10in
    ...

An example for elliptical diaphragm specification would be

  Def_Driver  'Drv1'
    WD=100mm
    HD=160mm
    Ellipse
    ...

Diaphragm Models

ABEC features several compact transducer-models, such as Driver. Driver may have different types of motors and mechanical components but the mechano-acoustical coupling is identical. In Driver there are two models available:

  1. Front and rear area are different in size
  2. Front and rear area are identical in size

It can be shown that, if SD1 = SD2, we can model a four-pole instead of a five-pole (which ABEC does automatically). For a four-pole-Driver there is one node for the front and the other node represents the rear side of the diaphragm. "p" in the circuit to the right is the pressure difference between the front and the rear.

(1) ABEC couples the Lumped Element to the Boundary Element analysis with the help of the effective diaphragm area SD (beside other parameters). In this scenario diaphragm areas are specified in the BE-script and there are DrvGroup= numbers in the LE-script, which organize the logistic. Front and rear of the diaphragm are typically different.

(2) ABEC also simulates pure Lumped Element scripts, i.e. without the BEM-part. In this case we need to specify the diaphragm area in the LE-script. In this case of pure LE-models, the Driver-component allows to specify only the diaphragm under the assumption that front and rear area are identical.

(3) ABEC performs also a mixed simulation, where, for example, the front side of the diaphragm is modeled with the help of the BEM and the rear of the diaphragm is modeled with the LEM. In this case we have two scenarios:

  1. We do not specify a diaphragm size in the LE-script. We assume the same size at the rear, and ABEC transfers the diaphragm dimensions from the BE-script to the LE-part and uses it also for the rear of the diaphragm.
  2. Additionally to the BE-specification we enter a diaphragm-dimension in the LE-script. For example Def_Driver; DrvGroup=1001; dD=24in; ... In this case the BEM-specification goes to the front side and the LEM-specification goes to the rear of the diaphragm.

(4) ABEC allows also for another mixed simulation. The analysis is fully coupled as in case (1) but some transducers are not coupled to the BEM. For example, a loudspeaker band-pass cabinet where we model only the exterior with the help of the Boundary Element Method. The interior may be modeled with LEM only. In this case the driver-diaphragm is not in touch with the BE-model and needs a specification of diaphragm parameters (similar to case 2).

Examples

The mechano-acoustical coupling needs to be expressed as one or multiple lumped diaphragm areas. For the general case you can use the Coupler component in order to model the diaphragms. For standard configurations, such as the loudspeaker, there are convenient compact electro-acoustic transducer-components, such as the Driver.

Loudspeaker with a Cone-Shaped Diaphragm

There can be distinguished several areas:

(1) To the front (dust-cap + cone + surround).

(2) To the rear (spider + cone + surround).

(3) Behind the dust-cap.

Often the the model is simplified by assuming identical size of area for the front and rear sides. The area behind the dust-cap is typically assumed to be part of the system and therefore part of the mechanical parameters.

Chapter Electro Dynamic Components demonstrates a script of a model for an electro-dynamic loudspeaker with fundamental lumped elements. This script also contains a second System with a compact component for comparison.

Compression Driver

The concave compression driver has a diaphragm, which features three parts, all of which move with the same velocity.

(1) Compression chamber

(2) Small ring-shaped chamber under suspension

(3) Rear diaphragm

Here, often, diaphragm (1) and (2) are acoustically coupled via a duct-system along the voice-coil, which features masses and springs with values are comparable to the mass of the diaphragm. This in turn yields complex resonance patterns.

See paper [28] for a detailed discussion and a lumped element model.

Dome Tweeter

The dome-tweeter is usually modeled with the front diaphragm only. The rear of the dome is regarded as part of the speaker and its effects are part of the TS-parameters (see also component Speaker).

Vibrating Diaphragms

ABEC does process velocity-distributions with the help of section VibFile. Here the distribution may be measured or stems from another simulation. Important to note in this context is that all integrals, which form the lumped diaphragm area include the distribution (see chapter Derivation). However, there is no feedback of the acoustics onto the particular mechanical vibration-pattern. Hence, regardless of the acoustic response the distribution is the same. Normally, in air-born models there is no strong coupling. Under water this is different, and we cannot make this assumption so easily.
