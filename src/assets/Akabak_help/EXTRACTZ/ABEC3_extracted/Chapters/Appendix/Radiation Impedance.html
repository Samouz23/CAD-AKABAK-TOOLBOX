Zast is the acoustic radiation impedance from DrvGroup=t to DrvGroup=s. Hence, DrvGroup=t is the source, which radiates and creates a sound pressure pt at the receiving diaphragm of DrvGroup=s. ν is the normalized normal velocity-distribution of the receiving diaphragm (DrvGroup=s) (The bar indicates conjugate complex).

S0 means integration of the boundary surface. S's is the effective radiation area.

For the self-radiation impedance s = t, otherwise we have a mutual radiation impedance.


"Lumped" means a power-mean-value over the radiating area of a Driving Group. For details, see [9]. The lumped Zast is not localized anymore. For example for a loudspeaker membran Zast can be measured at the point of mechanical driving. This point is typically there where the voice coil is attached to the cone.

The mechanical equivalent of the lumped radiation impedance is

zmst = zast·S's2

The radiation impedance is a spectrum. The signature of the curve has typical features, which are important for the design of electro-acoustic devices, such as horns, for example. One such feature is that the radiation impedance for free radiating devices approaches Z0 = ρ·c at high frequencies with Z0 the radiation impedance of a free traveling plane wave.

Hence, it is practical to view the curve normalized to Z0 and to the effective radiation area S's, so that the asymptotic value is one at high frequencies:

zst = zast·S's/(ρ·c)

The lumped radiation impedance is related to the acoustic power:

with lumped velocity at driving point vs. For uniform velocity distribution ν = 1 for all points on S and the power-formula becomes

Past = 1/2·zast·vs2·Ss2

Hence, for free radiation and for the self-impedance (s=t) we have the relation that the larger the real part of zast the higher the acoustic output for a loudspeaker.

For internal acoustics we observe a high value of the real part of zast at enclosure modes. The level of this value indicates the amount of damping.

The mutual cases of Past represents the acoustic energy exchange between diaphragms.

In general the imaginary part of zast is related to blind energy (the one which potentially goes into resonance). If it is positive then we have an acoustic mass. If zast is negative then it represents an acoustic stiffness.

Discussion of some Radiation Impedance Curves

The above diagram displays the normalized specific radiation impedance of a diaphragm of dD=2in in an infinite baffle (one side). With this diameter the wave-number-factor k·r = 1 at f = 2150 Hz.

The red curve is of a piston, the blue of a concave shaped dome and the green of a convex shaped dome. At high frequencies all curves converge to a value of one, which corresponds to Z0 = ρ·c. At very low frequency the radiation impedance goes to zero. At mid-band the wavelength is comparable to the dimension of the radiator. Here the radiation impedance typically displays its most interesting features. The shape of the curves depend on the details of the diaphragm (and also on its velocity distribution).

The real-part, which belongs to the piston (red) quickly raises without too much of an overshot. Its imaginary part is mass-like throught the frequeny band.

The curves belonging to the convex shaped diaphragm (green) are similar to those of the piston, however it typically features a more smooth transition band of the real-part.

The concave shaped diaphragm typically features the strongest overshot of the real part (blue). Remember that the acoustic power is proportional to the real-part. The imaginary part has frequency bands, where it becomes negative. This indicates a spring type radiation reactance caused by the cavity, which is formed by the diaphragm. If the depth of the diaphragm is enlarged, it would finally converge to a horn and then to a duct. In this case the ripples of the radiation impedance would correspond to the standing waves within the pipe, and would be more pronounced.
