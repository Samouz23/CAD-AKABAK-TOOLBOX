Integration points and receiving point

For applying the Boundary Element Method (BEM) the surface surrounding the field needs to be divided in finite elements. In ABEC constant pressure elements of triangular shape are used for solving the Helmholtz Integral. Any other shape specified is transformed into triangular patches. During solving the receiving point is the point of gravity of another triangle. The solver calculates unknown velocities and surface pressures according to the standard way of solving Integral Equations. Hence, for the solver the structure "looks" like a cloud of points, which are the center of the triangles.

At the subsequent observation stage, i.e. when an arbitrary sound field is calculated, the Green-functions of each triangle are integrated again and then weighted by the normal velocities and surface pressures as calculated at the solving stage.

At observation of a Field we want to calculate values at points in space. In order to distribute these points and to render values as a field-plot we need a mesh, too. For this ABEC makes use of the same mesh-generator as used for the Boundary Element Method. This means also, that section Field provides all meshing-parameters. The only difference to the boundary element mesh is that for field-calculation values are not calculated at the center of the triangle but at its vertices.


Type of Mesh

ABEC offers two types of mesh-generation: Bifurcation and Delaunay. Bifurcation is a technique where each specified quadrilateral and triangle is split into two new items in an attempt to yield a regular shape and simultaneously satisfy the specified mesh-criteria. Delaunay is an advanced schema [8], which triangulizes any shape directly in a most regular way. For most elements in ABEC the mesh-type can be selected and there are optional control parameters, as explained at parameter Meshing=.

Mesh Density

Before solving, ABEC investigates the mesh as specified and will cut-down elements until specified criteria are met. Main criterion is the side-length of the mesh-triangles, which can be specified with the help of EdgeLength= or indirectly via MeshFrequency=.

High Frequencies

At high temporal frequencies the wave-field is typically formed more complex due to interferences. For example, the acoustic field may show stronger variations at diffraction edges or at high modal density, whereas the field may be more regular across larger flat structures.

Basically each finite element should be small enough so that pressure-variations across the element can be regarded small. Here, we are in a dilemma because there is no reliable measure a priory.

Low Frequencies

For low frequency analysis the frequency criterion via MeshFrequency= may yield too a coarse mesh. In this case it is better to explicitly specify the parameter EdgeLength=, which controls the auto-mesher to generate triangles with sides smaller than the value of EdgeLength.

Opposite Walls

In BEM there is in principal a small exclusion zone around each radiating element within which the sound-field becomes undefined. In theory this zone is infinitesimal small but because we deal with finite elements the size of this zone is finite as well. Normally the auto-mesher makes the elements small enough so that in turn the mutual distance is large enough compared to the wave-length. However, elements can also be too close when, for example, a narrow duct is modeled. Elements of opposite walls may then violate this criterion. Another case is elongated triangles where the distance of the center points is much smaller then the length of the sides. In general, picture a small halo over each triangle having a radius of the inscribed circle. In a good model there is no intersection of these hemi-spheres. Narrow ducts need to be modeled with a higher resolution (see MeshFrequency= or EdgeLength=).

Overlapping Elements

One of the most encountered pitfalls is the overlapping of elements. If two elements occupy the same spot in space and belong to the same sub-domain then the solver cannot yield exact results because of ambiguity.

Holes and Gaps

The Boundary Element Method assumes a complete boundary, and the sub-domain should enclose a valid volume. The exterior domain (ElType=Exterior) forms only an apparent exception because here the domain is "closed" analytically by assuming Sommerfeld radiation condition at infinity.

However, one of the advantages of the Boundary Element Method is that the boundary-mesh does not need to be water-tight. The BEM can still give satisfactory results if the mesh shows small holes and gaps. The reason for it is that for a sub-domain all elements are inter-connected, i.e. the method is a holistic approach.

Subdomains

ABEC allows sub-domain modeling. With respect to meshing each sub-domain should be regarded independently, i.e. it should form a volume surrounded by the boundary. Any triangle generated should be uniquely belong to a single sub-domain. Here, the only exception are the interfaces, which triangles belong to exactly two sub-domains.

Meshing at Observation Stage

The Field-section allows to display the field of the sound pressure, either as level, amplitude, real-part, imaginary-part or as phase.

In order to produce the field, ABEC needs to generate a host of observation points and associated triangles for plotting. ABEC makes use of its meshing engine to produce these points. This means you can use parameters such as Meshing= and MeshFrequency= or EdgeLength= to control the density of these points.

At observation stage it is also difficult to give a rule of thumb for the mesh-density. The mesh-density should be high enough to avoid spatial aliasing. The only way in order to find proper values is by experimentation.

When calculating the pressure field it might happen that some receiving points are close to a boundary. ABEC is testing for this case but cannot fully rule out arte-facts, which stem from the fact that the pressure jumps across a boundary yielding the associated display-triangles invalid. For high quality displays we recommend to craft the display-planes so that they not cut through a boundary. Also a little offset helps.

At observation ABEC determines the sub-domain of a receiving point numerically by applying also a Boundary Element technique. If the boundary element mesh (not the observation mesh) is specified too coarse this technique may yield wrong results. Main indicator for this error are randomly invalid field-plot elements. Often a slight increase of mesh-density helps for producing stable results.
