Ma kg/m4 Acoustic mass

This two-pole component installs an acoustic mass in the network. This element can be used for modeling acoustic structures if the wavelength is large compared to the dimensions. In modeling acoustic structures, the acoustic mass is practical for taking into account effects such as obstructions, bends, etc. (see examples below).

The acoustic impedance is

Za = jω·Ma

In order to understand the acoustic mass, imagine an air-volume being accelerated and not compressed. The dual case, i.e. compression, would be an acoustic compliance.

Formula

The name of the parameters are equal to the formula-identifiers:

Parameter
Ma Mass

Derivation

The acoustic mass is derived from an open duct. If we ignore cross-modes the acoustic impedance of an open duct is:

Za = jρc/SD·tan(k·L)

with air-density ρ, speed of sound c, cross-sectional area of the duct SD, wave-number k = ω/c and length of the duct L.

At very low frequencies, only the first term of the Taylor-expansion of the tan-function may be used and Za = jω·Ma, where Ma is the acoustic mass:

Ma = M/SD2 = ρ·L/SD

with mechanic mass M of the air in the duct.

End Correction

If measured the acoustic mass of the duct appears somewhat greater because of the radiation-impedance present at both ends of the duct. The imaginary part of this radiation impedance forms an air-load, which equivalent acoustic mass Ma' should be added to Ma. At very low frequencies below fD = c/(π·dD) the air-load for each side is approximately [16]:

Ma' = 0.54·ρ/dD = 0.65/dD The duct terminates at a large baffle.
Ma' = 0.39·ρ/dD = 0.5/dD The duct terminates freely.

Alternatively the acoustic mass can be read from the observation of the Radiation Impedance with Domain=Acoustic and Calc Mass checked. See also chapter Radiation Impedance.

See also at component Duct parameter Len. There should be included a Ma' for each side. The procedure is called end-correction. In extreme cases the acoustic mass may consist only, or predominantly, of the end-correction, for example in the case of a simple hole in a thin wall.

Note, that we do not implement Ma' if the radiation impedance is provided by the BE-Method.

At high frequencies, the wavelength is small and the given equations for Ma may result in a large error. In this case it is better to replace the lumped element by the Duct element or by using the Boundary Element Method.

Examples

Acoustic masses occur not only in passages and ducts, but also in duct systems if the cross-section changes or obstructions are present. Also the holes of a mesh or within damping material induces acoustic mass. Finally any vibrating material may be expressed as an equivalent acoustic mass.

The following formulae are approximations for the frequency range in which the wavelength is large. If, for example, you need a value of the effect of a constriction, a bend, etc. These examples may form a suitable base for an estimation.

Duct with Constriction

Two ducts with identical cross-section have between them a thin wall with a hole of diameter dL. Let the area of the hole be much smaller than the cross-sectional area of the duct. The dimensions of the duct are also much smaller than the wavelength.



According to [15] the acoustic mass is given by

Ma = ρ/dL

with air-density ρ. Thus, with ρ=1.2kg/m3 and dL=20mm this results in Ma=60kg/m4. In the acoustic network Ma is in series with the output of the first duct and the input of the second.

Where HD is the height of the rectangular duct in meters. The mass is dependent only on the cross-section in the plane of the bend. The acoustic mass increases with the sharpness of the bend. In addition there is an acoustic resistance and a compliance.
